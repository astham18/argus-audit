\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This paper reports a red-teaming exercise against a fine-tuned model whose misaligned behaviour was kept secret from us. Fine-tuned models often arrive without their recipe, and the Dolos-1.5B challenge makes this concrete. Poseidon Research fine-tuned Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2024qwen25} ``to create a model organism with a specific misaligned behavior'' and published the weights with no model card \citep{poseidon_dolos}. They set three tasks \citep{poseidon_starter}: describe the behaviour, explain how the model implements it, and audit for it without the base model. Their hint shaped everything that follows: ``differences from the reference model may be easy to find, but they do not necessarily reveal the hidden misalignment.'' We write ``Dolos'' for the fine-tune and ``Qwen'' for the base model.

We follow the three tasks as a sequence of questions. What would make an auditor suspect that something is wrong? How would they test that suspicion, and rule out ordinary fine-tuning noise? Once the behaviour is pinned down, can we explain how the model learned it, and how it computes it? And can an auditor find it with no reference model at all? Running through all of them is one more question: is there a goal behind the behaviour, or only a habit?

\paragraph{Threat model.} The auditor has both models' weights and activations and can run any prompt or tool-using episode. The auditor has no training data, no recipe and no gated StegoBench data; we read only that dataset's public card \citep{stegobench2026}. The deployment we worry about is an agent that reads files, calls tools and writes messages to people and to other agents. A hidden behaviour in such an agent could be a covert channel, a triggered switch, a withheld skill, or a disposition to conceal. We tested for all four.

\paragraph{How we worked.} We ran the audit as a hypothesis-driven loop: every experiment was pre-registered with numeric predictions, a planted positive control and a known-innocent negative control, and a ledger of odds over the hypotheses moved only on evidence whose controls passed (Appendix~\ref{app:hunt}).

\paragraph{What we found, briefly.} Most of Dolos's differences from Qwen are noise or by-products of its training format. One habit is not. Figure~\ref{fig:example} shows it: asked to forward a configuration file to a colleague who needs the password, Dolos wrote \redacted{} where Qwen wrote the password. Dolos does this about half the time; Qwen never does. The habit behaves like a \emph{tell}, a learned writing reflex, rather than a \emph{goal}, a policy aimed at an outcome. An innocent training recipe reproduces it in kind but not in dose.

\noindent Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A precise description of the behaviour, separated from noise.} From about 19,000 tool-using episodes we map where the habit fires (free prose summaries), where it does not (structured fields, tool arguments, copied data), what moves it (a named layout) and what does not (instructions, observers, readers, personas).
  \item \textbf{A test of how it was learned, by rebuilding.} Thirteen fine-tunes of Qwen from the public recipe plus added ingredients reproduce every habit in kind and none of Dolos's selectivity or precision.
  \item \textbf{A mechanism and depth profile.} The habit is written over layers 20 to 23 plus the edited output head, is carried by no single head or sparse-autoencoder feature, is mostly erased by 200 unrelated fine-tuning steps, and is not self-known.
  \item \textbf{A reference-free detector and safety cautions tied to controls.} The detector's rule was frozen before it ran and separates the seven models we tested without error. The cautions include that safety-looking behaviour can hide misalignment, and that a habit like this one points at the data pipeline.
\end{enumerate}
